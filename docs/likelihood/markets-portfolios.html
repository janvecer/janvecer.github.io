Portfolio choice

Beliefs specify a target; trading determines what can be implemented

An information-theoretic guide to portfolio choice: beliefs, utility, attainable distributions, log growth, and the connections to mean–variance analysis and hedging.

Portfolio choice has three inputs: what you believe, what you want, and what you can trade. Information theory provides a common way to describe them. Beliefs give a probability distribution; preferences select a payoff; the asset menu determines which payoff distributions are attainable.

Start with one bet and the payoff–probability correspondence if these ideas are new. This page maps the next steps.

1. Specify the target

For expected log growth, investor beliefs \(\mathbb P^M\) are the target. With pricing law \(\mathbb P^Y\), the ideal unit-cost payoff is

\[ L_M=\frac{d\mathbb P^M}{d\mathbb P^Y}. \]

It pays more in outcomes to which the investor assigns relatively more probability. This is a full-market benchmark; the required state claims may not trade.

Other preferences can select another target. In a complete market, utility maximization chooses a payoff \(G^U\), which induces \(\mathbb P^U=G^U\mathbb P^Y\). That payoff is log-optimal under its own induced law. The utility page explains CRRA, fractional Kelly, and why a constrained utility problem generally must still be solved using its original utility objective.

Hedging starts from a different input: a given nonnegative claim \(H\) with price \(H(0)=\mathbb E^Y[H]>0\). Its normalized payoff induces

\[ \frac{d\mathbb P^H}{d\mathbb P^Y}=\frac{H}{H(0)}. \]

The claim supplies the target rather than an investor forecast. See pricing and hedging.

2. Determine the attainable set

Suppose \(R_1,\ldots,R_K\) are nonnegative unit-cost traded returns with \(\mathbb E^Y[R_j]=1\). A long-only allocation \(a_j\geq0\), \(\sum_j a_j=1\), produces

\[ \begin{aligned} G_a&=\sum_{j=1}^K a_jR_j,\\ \mathbb P^{V_a}&=\sum_{j=1}^K a_j\mathbb P^{X_j}. \end{aligned} \]

Thus long-only portfolios form a convex set of induced probability distributions. Additional position limits shrink the set. Intermediate rebalancing can enlarge the set of terminal payoffs relative to static trading; the available information and trading dates are part of the market specification.

The simplest illustration needs only three outcomes. In the incomplete football market, cash and a Win claim can match the desired Win probability but cannot independently choose the Draw/Loss split. The trinomial model shows the same geometry with a stock and cash.

3. Measure the cost of imperfect implementation

For a positive unit-cost payoff \(G_a=d\mathbb P^{V_a}/d\mathbb P^Y\), the central identity is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E^M[\log G_a] &=D(\mathbb P^M\Vert\mathbb P^Y)\\ &\quad-D(\mathbb P^M\Vert\mathbb P^{V_a}). \end{aligned} \]

Attainable log growth equals ideal information minus the KL gap. The first term is fixed once the beliefs and pricing law are specified. Maximizing expected log growth is therefore exactly the same as minimizing the second term over the attainable set. Complete markets can remove the gap; incomplete markets need not.

For finitely many states, this is a concave optimization problem in the portfolio weights:

\[ \max_{a\in\Delta_K}\sum_i\mathbb P^M(i) \log\!\left(\sum_j a_jR_j(i)\right). \]

The portfolio laboratory lets you change the target and the asset distributions and observe the resulting projection. The finite-state examples use positive pricing probabilities. A zero payoff in a state of positive subjective probability gives expected log growth \(-\infty\); the framework page states the support conditions for the general identity.

NoteWhich parts depend on the pricing law?

Keep beliefs, traded returns, and feasible portfolios fixed. Any equivalent pricing law \(Q\) that prices those returns gives

\[ \begin{aligned} D(\mathbb P^M\Vert QG_a) &=D(\mathbb P^M\Vert Q)\\ &\quad-\mathbb E^M[\log G_a]. \end{aligned} \]

Changing \(Q\) shifts all exact KL gaps by the same constant. The optimal portfolio and attained expected log growth stay unchanged, while the ideal benchmark and absolute gaps can change. Quadratic approximations need not preserve this invariance. See the comparison of hedging criteria.

The negative-binomial example makes the distinctions quantitative. Rebalancing at every binary step can implement the ideal likelihood payoff; trading only before and after the completed shock leaves a positive gap. That gap is the value, in expected log growth, of the unavailable rebalancing.

NoteNotation: beliefs, prices, and implemented payoffs

All returns are measured in units of a strictly positive reference asset \(Y\). Its own return is one, so interest and reinvestment are included in the choice of unit.

Symbol Role
\(\mathbb P^M\) Investor’s beliefs about outcomes
\(\mathbb P^Y\) Pricing law in reference-asset units
\(\mathbb P^X\) Law induced by the traded stock
\(\mathbb P^V\) Law induced by the implemented portfolio
\(\mathbb P^U\) Law induced by a utility-selected payoff
\(R=d\mathbb P^X/d\mathbb P^Y\) Stock’s gross return
\(L_M=d\mathbb P^M/d\mathbb P^Y\) Ideal payoff for the belief
\(V_w=1+w(R-1)\) Unit-cost stock-and-cash payoff

For \(0\leq w\leq1\), \(\mathbb P^{V_w}=w\mathbb P^X+(1-w)\mathbb P^Y\) is a convex mixture. Outside that range, admissibility still requires nonnegative wealth in every relevant state. In incomplete markets, the chosen pricing law need not be unique.

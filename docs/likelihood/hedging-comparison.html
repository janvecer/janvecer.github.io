One mismatch, three criteria

How trading restrictions separate exact information loss from quadratic hedging

A worked geometric-shock example comparing exact KL, its target-centered quadratic approximation, and ordinary quadratic hedging, with an interactive loss explorer.

The desired payoff can be clear even when trading cannot produce it. The investor supplies a target distribution; stock and cash span a smaller set of distributions. The choice of loss determines which attainable portfolio comes closest.

This page continues the binomial example and the negative-binomial trading clock. All payoffs are expressed in units of the reference asset \(Y\) and normalized to initial cost one.

The same binary tree, a different trading rule

In the binary model, stock returns are \(u=3/2\) and \(d=1/2\). The up probabilities are \(1/2\) under the pricing law and \(2/3\) under the investor’s beliefs. Rebalancing a stock fraction \(w=2/3\) after every step produces the desired likelihood payoff.

Now let one trading period contain \(G\) down moves followed by the first up move. You may trade before and after the completed shock, but not during it. For \(g=0,1,\ldots\),

\[ \begin{aligned} q_g&=\mathbb P^Y(G=g)=2^{-(g+1)},\\ m_g&=\mathbb P^M(G=g)=\frac23\left(\frac13\right)^g. \end{aligned} \]

The stock return and desired payoff are

\[ \begin{aligned} R_g&=\frac32\left(\frac12\right)^g,\\ L_{M,g}&=\frac{m_g}{q_g}=\frac43\left(\frac23\right)^g. \end{aligned} \]

The stock’s law is \(p^X_g=q_gR_g=\tfrac34(\tfrac14)^g\). Holding a stock fraction \(w\) unchanged throughout the shock gives

\[ \begin{aligned} V_w&=1+w(R-1),\\ v_g(w)&=q_gV_w(g). \end{aligned} \]

This is an affine payoff in \(R\). The desired \(L_M\) is nonlinear in \(R\), so no single stock-and-cash holding matches it in every state. Strictly positive wealth in every state requires \(-2<w\leq1\). The explorer focuses on \(0\leq w\leq1\), which contains all three optima in the examples below.

At \(w=2/3\), the portfolio matches the probability of \(G=0\) and the total probability of \(G\geq1\). It does not match the individual outcomes inside that tail. This is why the binary solution need not solve the completed-shock problem.

Three ways to measure the remaining mismatch

Write \(e_g=v_g(w)-m_g\). Exact information loss is

\[ \begin{aligned} D(w)&=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(m\Vert v(w))\\ &=\sum_gm_g\log\frac{m_g}{v_g(w)}. \end{aligned} \]

It is exactly equivalent to maximizing expected log growth:

\[ \mathbb E^M[\log V_w]=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(m\Vert q)-D(w). \]

Two quadratic alternatives are

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{KL}_2(w) &=\frac12\sum_g\frac{e_g^2}{m_g}\\ &=\frac12\mathbb E^Y\left[\frac{(V_w-L_M)^2}{L_M}\right], \end{aligned} \]

\[ Q(w)=\sum_g\frac{e_g^2}{q_g} =\mathbb E^Y[(V_w-L_M)^2]. \]

The target-centered \(\operatorname{KL}_2\) is the second-order expansion of exact KL when the relative mismatch \((V_w-L_M)/L_M\) is small with suitable integrability. Ordinary quadratic hedging weights payoff errors using the pricing law. The two quadratic criteria have different denominators; exact KL also has a different, logarithmic loss.

NoteA local approximation can have a different optimum

The target approaches zero in the tail, while a portfolio with \(w<1\) retains a positive cash intercept. Relative errors can therefore be large even when absolute errors are small. There is no guarantee that minimizing \(\operatorname{KL}_2\) gives a better exact-KL fit than minimizing ordinary squared error.

The resulting portfolios

The quadratic hedge has the regression coefficient

\[ \begin{aligned} w_Q&=\frac{\mathbb E^M[R]-1}{\operatorname{Var}^Y(R)}\\ &=\frac{1/5}{2/7}=\frac7{10}. \end{aligned} \]

For this target, the other quadratic criterion is explicitly

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{KL}_2(w)&=\frac{24}{65}w^2-\frac35w+\frac14,\\ w_{\mathrm{KL}_2}&=\frac{13}{16}. \end{aligned} \]

Exact log growth is strictly concave. Its interior maximizer solves

\[ \mathbb E^M\left[\frac{R-1}{1+w(R-1)}\right]=0. \]

The solution is \(w_{\mathrm{KL}}\approx0.745331\).

What changes when the pricing law changes?

With only terminal stock and cash traded, many probability laws price those same payoffs. Keep the original probabilities for \(g\geq3\), and set

\[ q'_0=\frac{17}{32},\qquad q'_1=\frac5{32},\qquad q'_2=\frac3{16}. \]

Both \(q\) and \(q'\) sum to one and satisfy \(\mathbb E^Q[R]=1\). Hold beliefs \(m\), stock returns \(R\), and the portfolio menu \(V_w\) fixed. Relative to any such law,

\[ \begin{aligned} &D_{\mathrm{KL}}(m\Vert QV_w)\\ &\quad=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(m\Vert Q)-\mathbb E^M[\log V_w]. \end{aligned} \]

The exact decision and attained growth are invariant. The ideal payoff \(m/Q\), the absolute information benchmark, and the two quadratic criteria change. This comparison holds beliefs fixed; it does not hold a separately specified hedge target fixed while changing its valuation.

The gap has a trading interpretation

On the original binary tree, rebalancing to \(w=2/3\) at every binary step produces

\[ \frac43\left(\frac23\right)^G=L_M. \]

Its expected log gain per completed shock is \(0.0849495\). The best portfolio held unchanged during the shock earns \(0.0814623\). Thus

\[ 0.0849495-0.0814623=0.0034872 \]

is the expected log growth lost because intermediate rebalancing is unavailable. This difference between two attainable growth values does not depend on a chosen pricing law. Its equality to the absolute KL gap above uses the original law \(q\), which is fixed by pricing every step of the binary tree. The alternative \(q'\) prices the terminal stock and cash, but not those intermediate trading opportunities.

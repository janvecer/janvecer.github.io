The negative binomial model

Keep the likelihood construction. Change the information clock.

In the binomial model, time counts trials. Here it counts completed failure blocks: observe successive Bernoulli trials and stop a block at its first failure. Count the successes in each block, then add them.

The information driver changes, while the rule stays the same: two probability laws determine a price through their likelihood ratio.

A different count

Let \(G(j)\) be the number of successes before the failure ending block \(j\). The blocks are independent. Under \(\mathbb P^X\), each trial succeeds with probability \(p\in(0,1)\); under \(\mathbb P^Y\), it succeeds with probability \(q\in(0,1)\).

After \(n\) completed blocks,

\[ Z(n)=G(1)+\cdots+G(n). \]

This counts successes before the \(n\)-th failure. The book uses the convention

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb P^X(Z(n)=k)\\ &\quad=\binom{n+k-1}{k}(1-p)^n p^k, \end{aligned} \]

for \(k=0,1,\ldots\), and the same formula with \(q\) under \(\mathbb P^Y\). We write these laws as \(\mathrm{NB}(n,p)\) and \(\mathrm{NB}(n,q)\). Unlike the binomial count, \(Z(n)\) has no upper bound.

The likelihood ratio is the price

The combinatorial factor cancels between the two laws. Therefore

\[ \frac{X(n)}{X(0)} =\left(\frac pq\right)^{Z(n)} \left(\frac{1-p}{1-q}\right)^n. \]

The logarithm is affine in the accumulated count and the number of completed blocks:

\[ \log\frac{X(n)}{X(0)}=aZ(n)+bn, \]

\[ a=\log\frac pq, \qquad b=\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}. \]

One block can contain any nonnegative number of successes. Its price multiplier is \((p/q)^{G(j)}(1-p)/(1-q)\), whose expectation under \(\mathbb P^Y\) is one. Products over independent blocks therefore form a positive \(\mathbb P^Y\)-martingale; their reciprocals form a \(\mathbb P^X\)-martingale.

The interactive figure requires JavaScript. For \(p=0.60\), \(q=0.40\), the information per completed failure block is approximately \(0.20273\) nats, so after ten blocks the expected log return under \(\mathbb P^X\) is approximately \(2.02733\).

The distributions are shown on the same success-count axis. Their probabilities are not renormalized when the chart truncates the infinite tail. The simulation displays the price itself, starting from \(X(0)=1\), and the logarithms of exactly the same six paths under each measure. Paths are sampled at completed blocks; the individual trials inside a block are not shown. The count-to-price figure can also switch to log price.

The horizontal dashed line in the price figure is \(\mathbb E^Y[X(n)/X(0)]=1\). In the log figure the dashed lines show expected log prices. Exponentiating an expected log price does not give an expected price: rare large outcomes can keep the mean price at one even while most simulated market-measure paths decline. Extremely large prices are explicitly marked as off scale; the log figure retains their full range.

Information grows linearly in block time

Let \(D(n;p\Vert q)\) denote the divergence from \(\mathrm{NB}(n,p)\) to \(\mathrm{NB}(n,q)\). Since \(\mathbb E^X[Z(n)]=np/(1-p)\), expected log growth is

\[ \mathbb E^X\left[\log\frac{X(n)}{X(0)}\right] =D(n;p\Vert q). \]

Define information per failure block by

\[ I(p,q)=\frac{p}{1-p}\log\frac pq +\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}. \]

Then the entire horizon contributes exactly \(nI(p,q)\). This is linear information flow in the book’s clock. It is also the additivity of relative entropy across the independent geometric blocks; their sum \(Z(n)\) retains the likelihood ratio.

Under the pricing measure the direction reverses:

\[ \mathbb E^Y\left[\log\frac{X(n)}{X(0)}\right] =-nI(q,p). \]

When \(p=q\), both information rates are zero and the asset has the reference asset’s constant return.

The binomial connection: same observations, same likelihood

Suppose the realized data contain \(k\) successes and \(n\) failures. They could have come from an experiment with a fixed total of \(k+n\) trials, or from an experiment stopped at the \(n\)-th failure. For an unknown success probability \(\rho\), both likelihoods are proportional to

\[ L(\rho)\propto\rho^k(1-\rho)^n. \]

Their proportionality factor depends on the sampling design and the observed counts, but not on \(\rho\). Thus comparing \(p\) with \(q\) gives the same likelihood ratio in either experiment. This illustrates the likelihood principle: proportional likelihood functions carry the same likelihood-based evidence about the parameter.

The sampling distributions differ, so procedures that depend on unobserved outcomes, including conventional fixed-design p-values, can differ. The principle concerns the evidential content of the observed likelihood; it does not say that the two experiments have identical sampling properties.

In financial terms, evaluate the binomial wealth process at the trial when the \(n\)-th failure occurs. Its wealth agrees with the negative-binomial formula above. Here the negative-binomial index \(n\) counts failures, not total trials.

Wald’s identity explains the change of clock

Let \(\tau(n)=Z(n)+n\) be the total number of trials needed for \(n\) failures. Under \(\mathbb P^X\),

\[ \mathbb E^X[\tau(n)]=\frac{n}{1-p}. \]

The same entropy can be written as

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{NB}(n,p)\Vert\mathrm{NB}(n,q)) \]

\[ =\frac{n}{1-p}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\Vert\mathrm{Bern}(q)). \]

For these bounded one-trial log-likelihood increments and the integrable stopping time \(\tau(n)\), Wald’s identity says: expected accumulated information equals information per trial times the expected number of trials. There is no extra information created by the new clock.

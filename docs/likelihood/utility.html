Utility & fractional Kelly

Two ways to moderate a likelihood-ratio position

Understand how utility transforms an implied measure, how fractional Kelly keeps a reserve, and why the distinction needs more than two states.

The framework gives a direct payoff for a belief: invest in the likelihood ratio \(L_M=\mathbb P^M/\mathbb P^Y\). That position maximizes expected log growth under the stated belief. It does not answer how much wealth an investor wants to expose to that belief, or which other preferences she might have.

Two familiar responses fit into the same language. Expected utility chooses a transformed payoff. Fractional Kelly allocates part of the budget to the original likelihood-ratio payoff and keeps the rest in the reference asset.

All wealth on this page is measured in units of the reference asset \(Y\). The formulas first describe a complete, finite-state market with positive pricing probabilities. Restricted asset menus require a further optimization over attainable portfolios.

What utility maximization does

Let \(W\) be initial wealth and \(V(T)\) the terminal payoff. An investor with increasing, strictly concave utility \(U\) solves

\[ \max_{V(T)\geq0}\;\mathbb E^M[U(V(T))] \]

subject to the pricing constraint

\[ \mathbb E^Y[V(T)]=W. \]

Write \(p_i=\mathbb P^M(i)\) and \(q_i=\mathbb P^Y(i)\). An extra unit paid in state \(i\) contributes \(p_iU'(V(T,i))\) to expected utility and costs \(q_i\). At an interior optimum, the marginal utility per unit of cost is the same in every state with \(p_i>0\):

\[ U'(V^*(T,i))=\lambda\frac{q_i}{p_i}. \]

If \(I=(U')^{-1}\) is inverse marginal utility, then

\[ V^*(T,i)=I\!\left(\lambda\frac{q_i}{p_i}\right). \]

The budget fixes \(\lambda\). The resulting portfolio still induces a probability distribution,

\[ \mathbb P^U(i)=q_i\frac{V^*(T,i)}{W}. \]

Utility therefore supplies a rule for turning the investor’s belief \(p\) and the market’s prices \(q\) into the implemented measure \(\mathbb P^U\).

NoteConditions behind the formula

The inverse-marginal-utility formula applies when \(U\) is continuously differentiable and strictly concave, with \(U'(0+)=\infty\) and \(U'(\infty)=0\). In the finite-state setting, a positive budget determines the multiplier uniquely. A state with \(p_i=0\) contributes nothing to the objective; paying it a positive amount would consume budget, so the optimizer assigns it zero. Utility at zero is interpreted through the boundary limit, and states of zero subjective probability do not enter the expectation.

Log optimality under the selected law

Let \(G^U=V^*(T)/W\) and suppose it is positive. The payoff selected by utility can be written

\[ G^U=\frac{d\mathbb P^U}{d\mathbb P^Y}. \]

For any other positive unit-cost payoff \(G^V=d\mathbb P^V/d\mathbb P^Y\), the same identity used in the introduction gives

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E^U[\log G^U]-\mathbb E^U[\log G^V]\\ &\qquad=D(\mathbb P^U\Vert\mathbb P^V)\geq0. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(G^U\) is log-optimal under its own induced law. Utility has selected that law from the original beliefs, prices, and risk preferences. For log utility, \(\mathbb P^U=\mathbb P^M\); other utilities generally select a different law. This observation does not require the investor to replace their forecast with \(\mathbb P^U\).

CRRA: a power of the likelihood ratio

Constant relative risk aversion uses

\[ U_\gamma(x)=\frac{x^{1-\gamma}-1}{1-\gamma}, \]

with \(U_1(x)=\log x\). Set \(\alpha=1/\gamma\) and \(L_M(i)=p_i/q_i\). The optimal gross payoff is

\[ G^U(i)=\frac{L_M(i)^\alpha}{\sum_jq_jL_M(j)^\alpha}. \]

Its implied probabilities are

\[ \mathbb P^U(i)=q_iG^U(i). \]

At \(\gamma=1\), \(G^U=L_M\) and \(\mathbb P^U=p\): utility reproduces the full Kelly position. For \(\gamma>1\), the power moderates the likelihood ratios. The denominator is essential: it makes the payoff cost exactly one unit of initial wealth.

For positive \(p_i\) in every state, increasing \(\gamma\) eventually brings the implemented measure back to \(q\). This is normalized geometric interpolation between the two distributions.

For the binary example \(q=(1/2,1/2)\), \(p=(2/3,1/3)\), and \(\gamma=2\),

\[ \mathbb P^U(\text{Win})=\frac{\sqrt2}{1+\sqrt2} \approx0.5858. \]

The induced Win probability lies between the market’s \(0.5\) and the investor’s \(2/3\). The construction is normalized geometric interpolation; the cash reserve below instead gives an arithmetic mixture.

Fractional Kelly: keep a reserve

Invest a fraction \(f\in[0,1]\) of wealth in the full Kelly payoff and leave \(1-f\) in \(Y\). The gross terminal payoff is

\[ G^F(i)=(1-f)+fL_M(i). \]

Multiplication by \(q_i\) gives

\[ v^F(i)=(1-f)q_i+fp_i. \]

The original belief still determines the active position. The total portfolio’s measure is an arithmetic mixture because part of the budget stays in reserve. In particular,

\[ G^F(i)\geq1-f. \]

This is a terminal wealth floor in units of \(Y\). With \(f=0.25\), at least three quarters of initial wealth remains even if the active position pays zero. It is not a claim about purchasing power in another unit, or about every strategy called “fractional Kelly.” Here the definition is a static holding of the reference asset and the full Kelly claim, without repeated rebalancing to a fixed risky weight.

Compare the two portfolios

Start with \(\gamma=2\) and \(f=1/2\). Compare the state probabilities and the payoff floor. Keep the fraction linked to \(1/\gamma\) for a matched-parameter comparison, or unlink it to choose the reserve independently. Then increase risk aversion and try a belief that assigns zero probability to one state. The three-state view makes the difference between arithmetic and geometric interpolation visible.

The choice \(f=1/\gamma\) is a useful comparison, not a general equivalence. For beliefs close to prices, both gross payoffs have the same first-order expansion:

\[ G\approx1+\frac{L_M-1}{\gamma}. \]

Further from the market distribution, their shapes and downside protection can differ substantially.

NoteWhy two states can conceal the difference

With two states and strictly positive beliefs, both families cover the segment from \(q\) to \(p\) for \(\gamma\geq1\), including cash as a limit. One allocation can be matched by a different parameter in the other family. They generally disagree at the same numerical \(\gamma\). With three or more states, they generally follow different curves, so changing one scalar parameter need not match the portfolios.

NoteWhat happens when a belief is zero?

For every finite \(\gamma\), CRRA retains the zero wherever \(p_i=0\). Fractional Kelly with \(f<1\) assigns that state positive mass through the reserve.

Let \(B=\{i:p_i>0\}\). As \(\gamma\to\infty\), the CRRA probabilities converge to

\[ \mathbb P^U(i)\longrightarrow \frac{q_i\mathbf1_B(i)}{\sum_{j\in B}q_j}. \]

Thus the cash limit requires full subjective support. Otherwise the limit is the pricing distribution conditioned on the states the investor considers possible.

The information cost of moderation

Write \(D(P\Vert Q)\) for relative entropy and let \(v\) be either implemented distribution. The growth decomposition gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E^M[\log G]&=D(p\Vert q)\\ &\quad-D(p\Vert v). \end{aligned} \]

The difference from full Kelly is exactly the mismatch \(D(p\Vert v)\). That loss of expected log growth can be an intentional choice made for another objective or a wealth floor. It is measured under the stated belief; changing the belief can change the ranking.

When the asset menu is restricted

The formulas above solve the full-menu problem: each state’s payoff can be selected separately. With only a few traded assets, the chosen utility payoff may be unattainable.

Two distinct questions can then arise:

  • To maximize the original expected utility, solve \(\max_{G\in\mathcal A}\mathbb E^M[U(WG)]\) over the attainable, unit-cost payoffs \(\mathcal A\).
  • To approximate the full-menu target law, minimize \(D(\mathbb P^U\Vert\mathbb P^V)\) over the laws induced by \(G\in\mathcal A\). This maximizes \(\mathbb E^U[\log G]\).

These objectives need not select the same portfolio. They coincide for log utility under the original belief, and in special cases, but there is no general equivalence for other utilities. An already-selected positive constrained utility optimum is still log-optimal under its own induced law; that is a representation of the optimum, not a shortcut for finding it.

The portfolio-choice overview keeps the target, the available assets, and the optimization criterion separate.

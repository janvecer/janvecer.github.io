Discrete hedging

Match the next interval’s density with the assets you can trade

Start with a discrete call hedge and its wealth constraint, then compare conditional option, stock and cash densities in the normal model.

An option carries a probability density. A portfolio of stock and cash carries another. Hedging asks how closely the second can reproduce the first. Exact agreement means replication; a remaining difference records the exposures the available portfolio cannot match.

The options page plots distributions over observations at expiry. Here the question is local: what can a portfolio reproduce between now and the next trading date? All prices are in units of \(Y\), so the reference asset has value one.

A small example: the wealth constraint matters

In the geometric-shock model, \(R_g=\tfrac32(\tfrac12)^g\) and \(q_g=2^{-(g+1)}\). A strike-one call pays

\[ \begin{aligned} C&=(R-1)^+=\frac12\mathbf1_{\{G=0\}},\\ c&=\mathbb E^Y[C]=\frac14. \end{aligned} \]

Only the first outcome puts the call in the money. Among all stock-and-cash portfolios with the same cost \(c\), the unconstrained quadratic fit is

\[ \begin{aligned} H_Q&=\frac7{16}R-\frac3{16},\\ \mathbb E^Y[(C-H_Q)^2]&=\frac1{128}. \end{aligned} \]

It has negative terminal wealth whenever \(G\geq2\). The squared-error criterion alone permits that result. Such a portfolio cannot induce a probability law.

If terminal wealth must remain nonnegative, write \(H_w=c[1+w(R-1)]\). The call’s induced law concentrates all probability at \(G=0\). Exact KL selects the all-stock boundary:

\[ \begin{aligned} w_{\mathrm{KL}}&=1,\qquad H_{\mathrm{KL}}=\frac14R,\\ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbb P^C\Vert\mathbb P^{H_{\mathrm{KL}}})&=\log\frac43. \end{aligned} \]

Quadratic fitting with the same nonnegative-wealth constraint also selects \(H=R/4\), with squared error \(1/56\). This distinguishes the choice of loss from the choice of admissible portfolios. See one mismatch, three criteria for an example where exact KL and the two quadratic losses select different positive portfolios.

One trading interval

Suppose the stock is worth \(x=X(t)\) today, the call expires in \(\tau=T-t\) years, and the next rebalance is in \(h\) years, with \(0<h\leq\tau\). In the normal model, a standard normal shock \(z\) produces the stock multiplier

\[ R(h,z)=\exp\!\left\{\sigma\sqrt h\,z-\tfrac12\sigma^2h\right\}. \]

Under the pricing measure, the shock has density \(q(z)=\varphi(z)\). The stock’s conditional density is therefore

\[ p(z)=R(h,z)\varphi(z). \]

Both integrate to one: \(\mathbb E^Y[R(h,Z)]=1\). The plotted horizontal axis is the same standardized shock for every density. These are distributions over the next interval’s information, not histograms of hedging errors.

Write \(C(x,\tau)\) for the Black–Scholes call value at current stock price \(x\) and remaining maturity \(\tau\). At the next trading date, its value is \(C(xR(h,z),\tau-h)\). Consequently the call’s conditional density is

\[ c(z)= \frac{C(xR(h,z),\tau-h)}{C(x,\tau)} \varphi(z). \]

Before expiry, the numerator is the remaining option value, rather than its terminal payoff. At \(h=\tau\), it becomes \((xR(h,z)-K)^+\). Conditional pricing ensures that this density also integrates to one.

A target that includes a reserve

The explorer can hedge a call together with a fixed reserve \(B\geq0\) in \(Y\). Its current value is

\[ H=C(x,\tau)+B. \]

Its density over the next interval becomes

\[ c_B(z)= \frac{C(xR(h,z),\tau-h)+B}{H} \varphi(z). \]

The default uses \(B=K\): the terminal target is \((X(T)-K)^++K=\max(X(T),K)\), a stock position with a protected floor. This is also a put plus the stock by put–call parity.

Adding the reserve changes the target payoff as well as its price. It makes the target less concentrated in upside states; it does not supply a cost-free improvement to the original call hedge.

Spend a fraction \(w\) of the same budget \(H\) on stock and keep the rest in \(Y\). Hold that position until the next trading date. Its value and density are

\[ V(t+h,z)=H\{1-w+wR(h,z)\}, \]

\[ v(w,z)=\{1-w+wR(h,z)\}\varphi(z). \]

This is a cost-weighted mixture of the stock and reference densities. The share holding is \(\Delta(t)=Hw/x\); the cost fraction \(w\) and the number of shares \(\Delta(t)\) are different quantities.

Move the hedge toward the target

Start with the call plus a reserve equal to the strike, then press Fit the density. Change the time to the next rebalance and compare the two curves. Finally select Call only to see why the positivity constraint matters.

The interactive density comparison requires JavaScript. For a stock price of 100, strike of 110, annual volatility 30%, and one year remaining, the call costs about 8.14 and its Black–Scholes delta is about 0.433 shares. With a 110-unit reserve the target costs 118.14; the corresponding delta cost fraction is about 0.367.

The fitted position minimizes the forward relative entropy

\[ w^\star=\underset{0\leq w\leq1}{\arg\min}\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}(c_B\Vert v(w)), \]

\[ \begin{aligned} &D_{\mathrm{KL}}(c_B\Vert v(w))\\ &\quad=\int c_B(z)\log\frac{c_B(z)}{v(w,z)}\,dz. \end{aligned} \]

Zero divergence means identical densities, hence identical next-date values for the same initial cost. Positive divergence measures the remaining mismatch in nats. It is also the loss of expected log performance, evaluated under the target measure, relative to holding the target itself.

The optimization is convex in \(w\). The figure compares its best value with the chosen position over several interval lengths. Shorter intervals change the local problem: they do not simply add more points to the same terminal distribution.

Why the call-only fit reaches a boundary

In the normal model, the next stock multiplier can be arbitrarily close to zero or arbitrarily large. A position held unchanged over a finite interval must satisfy

\[ 1-w+wR\geq0 \qquad\text{for every }R>0. \]

With only stock and cash, that condition is exactly \(0\leq w\leq1\). Borrowing cash would give \(w>1\), and sufficiently large downward moves would then make the portfolio negative. Shorting the stock would fail in the other tail. A negative function cannot be used as a probability density.

For the default call without a reserve, Black–Scholes delta requires about 0.433 shares, costing about 43.34, while the call itself costs only 8.14. Its stock cost fraction is about 5.32: the rest is financed by borrowing. The finite-step positive-density fit cannot take that position. The Call only preset makes the restriction visible by putting the best admissible fit at the all-stock boundary.

This does not contradict continuous-time replication. A continuously rebalanced strategy changes its position as the stock moves. It is a different admissibility problem from a leveraged position left unchanged across a finite interval.

Where Black–Scholes delta reappears

For the call plus a fixed reserve, the derivative with respect to stock price is still the call delta:

\[ \Delta^{\mathrm{BS}}(t)=\Phi(d(1)). \]

\[ w^{\mathrm{BS}}=\frac{x\Phi(d(1))}{H}. \]

The options page defines \(d(1)\) and derives the Black–Scholes formula from two normal tail probabilities. When this cost fraction is strictly inside the admissible interval, the short-interval KL fit approaches the same delta. The default reserve is large enough for this condition to hold. Press Fit the density, then reduce \(h\) to see the fitted share holding approach the delta benchmark.

The book also develops two quadratic hedges. For a positive normalized target payoff \(L\) and an equal-cost approximation \(V\), the target-centered KL approximation minimizes \(\tfrac12\mathbb E^Y[(V-L)^2/L]\); ordinary quadratic hedging minimizes \(\mathbb E^Y[(V-L)^2]\). Their finite-step positions need not agree. Under the local regularity and interior conditions, they approach delta as the interval shrinks in the normal model. The direct KL fit shown here retains the full logarithmic objective and its positivity restriction. The mean–variance page explains the two quadratic geometries and the limits needed to connect them.

NoteWhat is being calculated

The lab solves one conditional trading interval at the current stock price. A strategy on a trading grid repeats that calculation after each observed move, using the new stock price and remaining maturity. The graphs do not claim to show a complete multistep replication or a realized hedge profit.

The integrals use deterministic numerical quadrature over standard normal shocks, with a separate breakpoint at the strike when the interval reaches expiry. The plotted range contains nearly all the probability mass for these controls; numerical integration uses a wider range. Zero target density contributes zero to forward KL. The normal family and volatility range keep the integration well behaved.

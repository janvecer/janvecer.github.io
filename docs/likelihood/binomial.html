The binomial model

The whole framework in two states

Each period has two outcomes, \(H\) and \(T\): an up move and a down move in our running example. Three laws have different jobs: \(\mathbb P^Y\) prices payoffs, \(\mathbb P^X\) is induced by the traded stock, and \(\mathbb P^M\) describes investor beliefs. The stock is the investor’s log-optimal holding only when \(\mathbb P^M=\mathbb P^X\).

Every identity on the framework page has an explicit two-state form. We use the same numbers as the Columbia presentation:

Outcome Market \(\mathbb P^Y\) Stock \(\mathbb P^X\) Belief \(\mathbb P^M\) Stock return \(R\) Desired payoff \(L_M\)
\(H\): up \(1/2\) \(3/4\) \(2/3\) \(3/2\) \(4/3\)
\(T\): down \(1/2\) \(1/4\) \(1/3\) \(1/2\) \(2/3\)

Here \(R=\mathbb P^X/\mathbb P^Y\) and \(L_M=\mathbb P^M/\mathbb P^Y\). All prices and returns are in units of the reference asset \(Y\); interest and discounting are absorbed in that choice. When \(Y\) is money delivered at the horizon, the reference security is the corresponding bond.

This is the first worked example of likelihoods and price dynamics: the two approaches describe the same market, but start with different primitives.

Two probabilities, two multipliers

Write \(p=\mathbb P^X(H)\) and \(q=\mathbb P^Y(H)\), with \(p,q\in(0,1)\). Likelihood-ratio pricing, \(X(1)/X(0)=\mathbb P^X/\mathbb P^Y\), gives the \(H\) and \(T\) multipliers directly (up and down, respectively, when \(p>q\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} u&=\frac{p}{q},\\ d&=\frac{1-p}{1-q}. \end{aligned} \]

The corresponding asset return is

\[ \frac{X(1)}{X(0)}= \begin{cases} u & H,\\ d & T.\end{cases} \]

There are two martingale conditions, one for each choice of numeraire. Under the reference-asset measure \(\mathbb P^Y\), the normalized price of \(X\) has expectation one:

\[ \begin{aligned} q\,u+(1-q)\,d &=p+(1-p)\\ &=1. \end{aligned} \]

Under the asset measure \(\mathbb P^X\), the normalized price of \(Y\) in units of \(X\) is the reciprocal return. It also has expectation one:

\[ \begin{aligned} p\,\frac1u+(1-p)\,\frac1d &=q+(1-q)\\ &=1. \end{aligned} \]

The first condition identifies \(q\) from nonconstant price multipliers; the second identifies \(p\). Thus \(p\) is the probability associated with the asset numeraire, not an independently supplied forecast. The investor’s forecast is \(m=\mathbb P^M(H)\). In the example, \(q=1/2\), \(p=3/4\), and \(m=2/3\).

The classical one-period model runs the other way. It postulates \(u\) and \(d\) and derives the pricing probabilities from these martingale conditions. Solving them gives

\[ \begin{aligned} q&=\frac{1-d}{u-d},\\ p&=\frac{u(1-d)}{u-d}=u\,q. \end{aligned} \]

The map \((p,q)\mapsto(u,d)\) sends \(\{0<q<p<1\}\) one-to-one onto \(\{0<d<1<u\}\). Read backwards, the classical no-arbitrage condition \(d<1<u\) corresponds to two interior probabilities ordered so that \(H\) is the up move. With \(p<q\), the price directions reverse: \(H\) is the down move and \(0<u<1<d\). When \(p=q\), \(u=d=1\): the asset has the reference asset’s return. In that degenerate case the return identifies neither probability, and the inverse formulas are undefined. Equal multipliers different from one imply deterministic arbitrage in units of \(Y\), under the frictionless borrowing and short-selling assumptions.

With two states, cash and a nonconstant asset span all payoffs, which is why these conditions identify the two measures uniquely. The trinomial model shows what changes when there are three states and only two traded assets.

Replication: shares and probability weights

The classical delta hedge and likelihood matching are the same calculation in different coordinates. To replicate terminal wealth \(V(1)\), hold \(\Delta\) shares of \(X\) and the remaining budget in \(Y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta&=\frac{V(1,H)-V(1,T)}{X(1,H)-X(1,T)},\\ B&=V(0)-\Delta X(0). \end{aligned} \]

The initial fraction of the budget invested in the stock is \(w=\Delta X(0)/V(0)\). Normalized terminal wealth and its induced probability law satisfy

\[ V_w=\frac{V(1)}{V(0)}=1+w(R-1). \]

\[ \mathbb P^V=w\mathbb P^X+(1-w)\mathbb P^Y. \]

For the target \(\mathbb P^V=\mathbb P^M\), the \(H\) equation gives

\[ \begin{aligned} w&=\frac{m-q}{p-q}\\ &=\frac{2/3-1/2}{3/4-1/2}=\frac23. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(\Delta=(2/3)V(0)/X(0)\), \(B=V(0)/3\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} V_{2/3}(H)&=\frac43,\\ V_{2/3}(T)&=\frac23. \end{aligned} \]

The line \(V_w=1+w(R-1)\) passes through both desired payoffs. With two states and two independent securities, the target is attainable exactly. The mixture is convex for \(0\leq w\leq1\); borrowing or short selling can extend it when terminal wealth remains nonnegative.

Every position on one curve

Write \(d(a\Vert b)\) for the relative entropy between Bernoulli laws with probabilities \(a\) and \(b\):

\[ \begin{aligned} d(a\Vert b) &=a\log\frac ab\\ &\quad +(1-a)\log\frac{1-a}{1-b}. \end{aligned} \]

The usual conventions apply: a zero-probability term contributes zero, while positive probability divided by zero gives infinite divergence.

Let the investor’s model be \(\mathbb P^M(H)=m\). With two scenarios a nonnegative portfolio \(V\) of positive price is determined, up to scale, by \(v=\mathbb P^V(H)\), the fraction of wealth spent on the \(H\) claim. The stock fraction \(w\) in the replication section gives

\[ v=wp+(1-w)q. \]

Thus the \(v\)-curve below and the payoff line \(V_w=1+w(R-1)\) describe the same positions in different coordinates. The portfolio’s expected log return under the investor’s model is

\[ \begin{aligned} g(v) &=m\log\frac{v}{q}\\ &\quad +(1-m)\log\frac{1-v}{1-q}. \end{aligned} \]

The information decomposition becomes

\[ g(v)=d(m\Vert q)-d(m\Vert v). \]

Three points on the curve have names. At \(v=q\) the investor holds the reference asset and \(g=0\). At \(v=p\) she holds the asset \(X\). At \(v=m\) the curve peaks at \(d(m\Vert q)\), and the shortfall of any other position is exactly \(d(m\Vert v)\). Every strictly positive portfolio is log-optimal for the model it implies, and for no other in this unconstrained two-state market.

In the running example, the replicated target earns

\[ \begin{aligned} g^*&=\frac23\log\frac43+\frac13\log\frac23\\ &\approx0.0566330\ \text{nats per trial}. \end{aligned} \]

Holding the stock alone earns \(\frac23\log(3/2)+\frac13\log(1/2)\approx0.0392610\). The investor and stock laws differ, so simply buying the stock does not maximize this investor’s expected log growth.

For \(\gamma\ge1\), risk aversion moves the position from \(m\) back toward \(q\), in one of two ways. Fractional Kelly keeps a cash reserve:

\[ \begin{aligned} f&=1/\gamma,\\ v_f&=(1-f)\,q+f\,m. \end{aligned} \]

CRRA utility instead tempers the measure. Set \(\alpha=1/\gamma\) and form the weights

\[ \begin{aligned} a_H&=m^\alpha q^{1-\alpha},\\ a_T&=(1-m)^\alpha(1-q)^{1-\alpha}. \end{aligned} \]

The optimal \(H\) weight is

\[ v_\gamma=\frac{a_H}{a_H+a_T}. \]

For \(0<m<1\) both families sweep the same segment between \(q\) and \(m\), including cash as a limit as \(\gamma\to\infty\). They generally traverse it at different rates: the same numerical \(\gamma\) can give different positions, while a change of \(\gamma\) can match them. With three or more states they generally trace different curves in the simplex, arithmetic against normalized geometric interpolation. Boundary beliefs give another distinction: set \(\mathbb P^M(H)=0\) in the figure. For every finite \(\gamma\), CRRA keeps that zero, while fractional Kelly with \(\gamma>1\) assigns positive weight through its cash reserve. The utility and risk-aversion page develops the general construction.

Many periods

Repeat the period independently and let \(S(n)\) count the \(H\) outcomes after \(n\) periods. Likelihood-ratio pricing at time \(n\) gives

\[ \frac{X(n)}{X(0)} =\Big(\frac pq\Big)^{S(n)} \Big(\frac{1-p}{1-q}\Big)^{n-S(n)}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \frac{X(n)}{X(0)}=u^{S(n)}d^{\,n-S(n)}. \]

The binomial coefficients in \(\mathbb P^X\) and \(\mathbb P^Y\) cancel. The price depends on the path only through the sufficient statistic \(S(n)\), and its logarithm is affine in it:

\[ \log\frac{X(n)}{X(0)}=a\,S(n)+b\,n. \]

Here the coefficients are

\[ \begin{aligned} a&=\log(u/d),\\ b&=\log d. \end{aligned} \]

Write \(D^{X,Y}(n)\) for the divergence between the laws of \(S(n)\) under the two measures. Independence gives

\[ D^{X,Y}(n)=n\,d(p\Vert q). \]

The investor’s desired likelihood payoff has the same form with \(p\) replaced by \(m\). It is implemented by rebalancing to \(w=2/3\) after each trial in our example, and its expected log growth under \(\mathbb P^M\) is \(n\,d(m\Vert q)=n\times0.0566330\).

This is linear information flow: independent observations add information, while wealth compounds multiplicatively.

Under \(\mathbb P^Y\) the price \(X(t)/X(0)\) is a nonnegative martingale with mean one. Ville’s inequality then gives, for every horizon \(n\),

\[ \mathbb P^Y\Big(\max_{t\le n}\frac{X(t)}{X(0)}\ge\frac1\alpha\Big)\le\alpha . \]

For \(0<\alpha<1\), the price path of \(X\) is therefore an anytime-valid test of the market’s model against the asset’s: one may stop the moment \(X\) has multiplied by \(1/\alpha\), and the probability of ever crossing under \(\mathbb P^Y\) is at most \(\alpha\). If \(p\ne q\), then under \(\mathbb P^X\) the long-run log growth is the strictly positive one-period divergence, so the threshold is eventually crossed with probability one over an unbounded horizon. If \(p=q\), wealth stays constant and no such rejection occurs.

Same observations, different trading opportunities

Now group the trials into cycles: down moves followed by the first up. If trading remains possible after every binary step, the same \(w=2/3\) strategy still implements the belief likelihood. If trading is permitted only between completed cycles, a single stock/cash position need not match every possible cycle outcome.

The negative-binomial model develops this change of clock. The comparison of portfolio criteria then measures the resulting information gap and compares exact KL, its quadratic approximation, and quadratic hedging.
